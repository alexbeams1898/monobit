# Limbo — canonical map (Inferno III + IV)

Objective reconstruction of Limbo as described in Dante's text.
Nothing here is Selva design; this is the source material. Read
`docs/design/limbo.md` for how Selva diverges from it.

Citations use Longfellow line numbers from
`docs/reference/inferno_longfellow.txt`. Italian original is at
`docs/reference/inferno_italian.txt` for terminology checks.

## Zones in descent order

### 1. Gate of Hell (Canto III opening)

- Inscription: "PER ME SI VA NE LA CITTÀ DOLENTE / PER ME SI VA NE
  L'ETTERNO DOLORE / PER ME SI VA TRA LA PERDUTA GENTE …
  LASCIATE OGNE SPERANZA, VOI CH'INTRATE" (III.1–9, Longfellow
  388–398).
- A gate with visible summit-mounted inscription, "in sombre colour."
- Dante and Virgil pass through it and enter the world of the dead.
- The gate is BEFORE Acheron. Not on the far side. Not the boundary
  of Limbo — the boundary of Hell entire.

### 2. The Vestibule of the futile (Canto III, between the gate and Acheron)

- **This is a distinct region between the gate and Acheron.** Often
  called "the Vestibule" or "the Ante-Inferno" in commentary. Dante
  doesn't name it; he just describes what fills it.
- **What is heard:** "sighs, complaints, and ululations loud /
  Resounded through the air without a star" (III.22–24, L 416–418).
  "Languages diverse, horrible dialects, / Accents of anger, words of
  agony, / And voices high and hoarse, with sound of hands" (III.25–27,
  L 420–422). The tumult "goes whirling on / For ever in that air for
  ever black, / Even as the sand doth, when the whirlwind breathes"
  (III.28–30, L 424–426).
- **Who is here:** the melancholy souls who "lived withouten infamy or
  praise" (III.36, L 434). Commingled with the "caitiff choir of
  Angels" who "have not rebellious been, / Nor faithful were to God,
  but were for self" (III.38–39, L 437–438). Neither Heaven nor Hell
  proper wants them.
- **Their punishment:** they chase a whirling banner ("a banner, /
  Which, whirling round, ran on so rapidly, / That of all pause it
  seemed to me indignant" — III.52–54, L 456–458) in an endless
  train. They are naked. They are "stung exceedingly / By gadflies
  and by hornets that were there. // These did their faces irrigate
  with blood, / Which, with their tears commingled, at their feet /
  By the disgusting worms was gathered up" (III.65–69, L 473–478).
- Dante recognizes "the shade of him / Who made through cowardice the
  great refusal" (III.59–60, L 465–466) — traditionally read as
  Pope Celestine V, who abdicated the papacy.

### 3. Acheron — the near shore (Canto III end)

- After the vestibule, Dante sees "People I saw on a great river's
  bank" (III.71, L 481). This river is Acheron.
- **The near shore is the accumulation point for damned souls
  arriving in Hell.** "All those who perish in the wrath of God /
  Here meet together out of every land" (III.122–123, L 549–550).
- The souls arrive already condemned, from all lands.
- **Charon** (III.82–83, L 496–497): "an old man, hoary with the
  hair of eld," in a boat, approaches shouting: "Woe unto you, ye
  souls depraved!" He is the ferryman.
- Charon's appearance: "the fleecy cheeks / Of him the ferryman of
  the livid fen, / Who round about his eyes had wheels of flame"
  (III.97–99, L 516–518); "with the eyes of glede" (III.109, L 532)
  — glowing coals.
- **Charon's function is compulsion.** Souls curse God, their
  parentage, humanity, and the time and place of their birth
  (III.103–105, L 524–526). They then "draw back, / Bitterly weeping,
  to the accursed shore" (III.106–107, L 528–529). Charon
  "Beckoning to them, collects them all together, / Beats with his
  oar whoever lags behind" (III.110–111, L 533–534). The souls
  ultimately throw themselves into the boat "at signals, as a bird
  unto its lure" (III.117, L 542) — but the machinery is Charon
  driving them.
- Charon refuses Dante passage: "By other ways, by other ports /
  Thou to the shore shalt come, not here, for passage; / A lighter
  vessel needs must carry thee" (III.91–93, L 508–510). Virgil
  overrules him: "It is so willed there where is power to do / That
  which is willed" (III.95–96, L 513–514).
- **How Dante crosses:** he doesn't row. An earthquake ("all the
  dusk champaign / Trembled so violently" III.130, L 560) and a
  vermilion light (III.134, L 565) overcome him and he collapses
  unconscious. He wakes on the far side.
- The river itself is called "the livid fen" (Longfellow's
  translation of "livida palude," III.98, L 517) — implying not
  clear water but a dark, muddy, drowned marsh.

### 4. Acheron — the far shore = the edge of Limbo (Canto IV opening)

- Dante wakes "upon the verge … / Of the abysmal valley dolorous,
  / That gathers thunder of infinite ululations" (IV.7–9, L 584–586).
  The far shore of Acheron IS the rim of the pit; the ululations
  from below are the sounds of all Hell.
- The abyss is "Obscure, profound … and nebulous, / So that by
  fixing on its depths my sight / Nothing whatever I discerned
  therein" (IV.10–12, L 588–590). One cannot see into the further
  circles from here.
- To enter Limbo proper, they descend: "Let us descend now into the
  blind world" (IV.13, L 592). Virgil goes first. This is the
  descent from the rim into Limbo, "the foremost circle that
  surrounds the abyss" (IV.24, L 606). **Limbo IS the first circle
  and it wraps around the entire abyss below.**

### 5. Limbo proper — the outer plain (Canto IV, first half)

- **Sound:** "Lamentations none, but only sighs, / That tremble
  made the everlasting air" (IV.26–27, L 609–610). This is the famous
  *d'i sospiri* — an atmosphere of sighs, not of screams.
- **Cause of the sighs:** "sorrow without torment" (IV.28, L 612).
  No active punishment. Only unresolved longing.
- **Population:** "crowds … that many were and great, / Of infants
  and of women and of men" (IV.29–30, L 613–614). Vast.
- **Why they're here** (Virgil explaining, IV.34–42, L 620–630):
  "they sinned not; and if they merit had, / 'Tis not enough,
  because they had not baptism / Which is the portal of the Faith
  thou holdest; // And if they were before Christianity, / In the
  right manner they adored not God; // For such defects, and not for
  other guilt, / Lost are we and are only so far punished, / That
  without hope we live on in desire."
- **The key phrase: "without hope we live on in desire"** —
  "sanza speme vivemo in disio" (IV.42). This is Limbo's defining
  condition. Not pain, not torment. Perpetual unfulfilled desire.
- **The Harrowing of Hell** (IV.52–63, L 644–658): Virgil recalls
  Christ ("a Mighty One, / With sign of victory incoronate") coming
  and taking Adam, Abel, Noah, Moses, Abraham, David, Israel, Rachel,
  "and others many" from Limbo up to Heaven. Before this event,
  "Never were any human spirits saved." Everyone left in Limbo now
  is virtuous but pagan or unbaptized — no Old Testament patriarchs
  remain.

### 6. The plain traversal (Canto IV middle)

- Dante and Virgil pass "onward through the forest, / The forest,
  say I, of thick-crowded ghosts" (IV.65–66, L 661–662). Metaphor:
  the wandering souls are dense enough to be a forest.
- "Not very far as yet our way had gone / This side the summit" (IV.67–68,
  L 664–665) — they haven't traveled deep into the circle before
  the next landmark.

### 7. The Fire and the Noble Castle (Canto IV second half)

- **The fire:** "a fire / That overcame a hemisphere of darkness"
  (IV.68–69, L 665–666). One fire, small enough to be a landmark,
  strong enough to define a lit hemisphere in the surrounding gloom.
  **This is the only light in Limbo.**
- **The privileged inhabitants:** "That honourable people held that
  place" (IV.72, L 670). They are Limbo's elite — souls whose earthly
  fame "wins grace in Heaven" (IV.78, L 678) enough to earn them a
  higher station within Limbo, though not release.
- **First arrivals:** Homer (with a sword/falchion in hand — IV.86,
  L 689), Horace, Ovid, Lucan — Virgil's four fellow classical poets.
  They welcome Virgil and, by extension, Dante ("they made me one of
  their own band; / So that the sixth was I" — IV.101–102, L 709–710).
- **Approach to the Castle:** "We came unto a noble castle's foot, /
  Seven times encompassed with lofty walls, / Defended round by a
  fair rivulet" (IV.106–108, L 716–718).
- **Castle features:**
  - **Seven walls,** concentric, all "lofty."
  - **A rivulet** (Longfellow: "fair rivulet"; original: "un bel
    fiumicello") as moat.
  - The rivulet "we passed over even as firm ground" (IV.109, L 720)
    — it parts / hardens for the worthy.
  - **Seven gates,** one per wall: "Through portals seven I entered
    with these Sages" (IV.110, L 721).
  - **A meadow of fresh verdure** ("un prato di fresca verdura,"
    IV.111, L 722) inside the walls.
  - Elevated ground: they withdraw "into an opening luminous and
    lofty" (IV.116, L 729) — the meadow itself is on high ground
    within the walls.
- **Castle inhabitants** (Canto IV names them; grouped by role):
  - **Trojan/Roman heroes** (IV.121–128, L 736–743):
    Electra (with attendants), Hector, Aeneas, Julius Caesar in
    armor "with gerfalcon eyes," Camilla, Penthesilea, King
    Latinus, his daughter Lavinia.
  - **Roman republicans** (IV.127–129, L 744–746):
    Brutus (who drove out Tarquin), Lucretia, Julia, Marcia,
    Cornelia.
  - **Alone, apart:** Saladin — "alone, apart, the Saladin" (IV.129,
    L 746). A twelfth-century Muslim sultan; Dante puts him among
    the virtuous pagans though technically post-Christian.
  - **Philosophers** (IV.130–144, L 748–767):
    Aristotle ("the Master … of those who know" — IV.131, L 749)
    is the presiding figure, with Socrates and Plato standing
    closest to him. Also Democritus, Diogenes, Anaxagoras, Thales,
    Zeno, Empedocles, Heraclitus. Then Dioscorides ("the good
    collector" of qualities — a botanist/pharmacologist), Orpheus,
    Cicero ("Tully"), Livy, Seneca, Euclid, Ptolemy, Galen,
    Hippocrates, Avicenna, Averroes ("who the great Comment made"
    — Averroes' commentary on Aristotle).
- **Comportment of the inhabitants:** "People were there with solemn
  eyes and slow, / Of great authority in their countenance; / They
  spake but seldom, and with gentle voices" (IV.112–114, L 724–726).
  Dignified, quiet, unhurried. This is Limbo's antithesis of the
  torment of the lower circles.

### 8. Departure from the Castle (Canto IV close)

- The "sixfold company" (Dante + Virgil + the four poets) splits:
  "Another way my sapient Guide conducts me / Forth from the quiet to
  the air that trembles" (IV.150–151, L 773–774). Virgil takes Dante
  out of the Castle back into the plain.
- Final line of the canto: "And to a place I come where nothing
  shines" (IV.151, L 776). Beyond the Castle's hemisphere of light,
  Limbo returns to complete darkness.
- Dante wakes in Canto V being judged by Minos at the descent into
  Lust (Circle II). Between "nothing shines" and Minos, Dante gives
  no description — the text elides the transition. The far edge of
  Limbo is where he meets Minos.

## Structural summary

**Traversal path, verified from the text:**

```
Gate of Hell (with inscription)
    ↓
Vestibule of the futile (whirling banner, gadflies, worms)
    ↓
Acheron near shore (souls waiting)
    ↓  [Charon compels; Dante crosses unconscious via earthquake]
Acheron far shore = rim of the abyss (dark, unfathomable)
    ↓  [descent into Limbo]
Outer plain of Limbo (crowds, sighs, no torment)
    ↓  [forest of thick-crowded ghosts]
Fire visible ahead (only light source)
    ↓
Noble Castle (seven walls, seven gates, rivulet-moat, meadow)
    │  — inhabited by classical / republican Roman heroes,
    │    Trojan royals, Saladin apart, philosophers around Aristotle
    ↓  [depart via "another way"]
Plain beyond the Castle ("place where nothing shines")
    ↓
Far edge of Limbo → Minos at the entry to Lust (Canto V)
```

**Geometry:**

- Limbo is **shaped like an annulus** — the "foremost circle that
  surrounds the abyss" (IV.24, L 606). It wraps around the pit below.
- **Light source topology:** ONE fire in the middle of Limbo, casting
  a hemispherical bright region around itself. Everywhere else is
  dark. When you leave the Castle's light, you're in darkness again.
- **The Castle sits within the fire's hemisphere** — the fire IS the
  Castle's fire (or on the Castle's grounds), and the two are
  co-located as a single lit region.
- **The Castle is on a high point** ("opening luminous and lofty" —
  the meadow inside is elevated).
- **The moat is a small stream** ("fiumicello"), not the river Acheron.
  Acheron is outside/above Limbo entirely; Limbo has its own moat.

**Population density:**

- The outer plain: enormous crowds, "many and great," "thick-crowded"
  enough to be a "forest of ghosts."
- The Castle inhabitants: ~30 named figures + implied companions.
  Elite subset.
- Ratio: the Castle is a small privileged enclave in a plain of vast
  anonymous multitude.

**Sound:**

- Vestibule (before Limbo): screaming, cursing, hornets, chaos.
- Limbo proper: sighs ("only sighs, / That tremble made the
  everlasting air"), no screams. Trembling air is the acoustic signature.
- The Castle interior: quiet dignified speech ("they spake but seldom,
  and with gentle voices").

**Temperature / weather:**

- Not specified for Limbo proper. Charon promises souls "eternal shades
  in heat and frost" (III.87, L 502) — Hell overall is temperature-
  variable but Limbo isn't given a temperature reading.

**Vegetation:**

- The plain: "forest, say I, of thick-crowded ghosts" — the "forest" is
  METAPHORICAL. The souls are dense as a forest. No actual trees are
  described.
- Inside the Castle: "meadow of fresh verdure" ("prato di fresca
  verdura") — literal fresh green grass. This is the ONLY vegetation
  described in Limbo.
- The rivulet-moat is presumably clean since it "parts for the worthy"
  and Dante compares crossing it to firm ground — implying not a
  swamp.

## What Dante does NOT describe

Worth noting explicitly, since the design will make choices in these
gaps:

- **The color of Acheron.** Only "livid" (livida) — pale-bluish-dark.
  No adjective for its motion, speed, or clarity.
- **The width of Acheron.** Dante is unconscious for the crossing;
  no traversal distance is given.
- **The wall material** of the Castle. "Lofty" but not stone / marble
  / brick. Not attributed.
- **Time of day.** Hell has no sun. The starless air is permanent.
- **How many souls total.** "Many and great" is the only measure.
- **What the wandering souls DO.** They wander, they sigh. No described
  activity beyond that.
- **The Castle's interior architecture.** Meadow is described; halls,
  rooms, seating are not. The philosophers stand or sit but no
  furniture is named.
- **Where inside Limbo the descent to Lust is.** Dante fades from
  "nothing shines" to Minos in Canto V; the geometry of the transit
  is elided.
- **Distance / travel time** at any point in the canto. Everything
  is "not very far" or unspecified.

These gaps are where Selva has room to make choices without
contradicting the source.
