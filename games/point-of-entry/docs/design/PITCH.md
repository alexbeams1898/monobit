# Point of Entry — the pitch

> **Status:** first draft, captured from the session it was invented in. Nothing
> here is locked; the point is to stop the good decisions evaporating. Working
> title.

## One sentence

**An exterminator takes a routine job at a house and finds something under it.**

## The elevator version

An action RPG — Zelda-ish combat crossed with Vampire Survivors, in a
Link-to-the-Past / Mother 1 aesthetic — about a blue-collar exterminator who is
assigned a job that unearths what is rotting the world. You find the holes the
vermin are coming through, seal them, and each one opens the way to the next. You
exterminate, come back up to town, spend, and go deeper. The things you kill get
stranger the further down you go: ants, then mice, then things that are neither.

Short — a year or less. Intended as a **showcase / pilot**, not a magnum opus.

## The tone, which is the whole thing

**A man filing a work order about the mouth of hell.**

The comedy and the horror are the same object, and neither undercuts the other:
the mundanity makes the horror land, and the horror makes the mundanity funny.

- **He is not funny on purpose.** He is *painfully* extermination-headed and
  mostly apathetic to the fact that things are getting evil around him. He kills
  demons without thinking twice, because it is part of the job he is working on.
- **The player is horrified while he is not.** That gap is where the tone lives.
  You see a thing that should not exist; he sees a callback and a materials cost.
- **He only ever comments in trade terms.** Never "what *is* that" — always
  "that's going to be a problem," "I've seen worse in a crawlspace," "that's not
  covered." Every line is an assessment. The character is a single stubborn frame
  applied to escalating input, which is why it never gets tiring.
- Register: absurdist and nonsensical up top, genuinely evil and blasphemous
  underneath. Body horror, gore, demons. **No cosmology** — see below.

**Not** a "diet Selva Oscura." Selva is solemn and Dantean at scale; this is a
man with a clipboard finding something he has no vocabulary for. Same
preoccupations, opposite register, and this one's job is to SHIP.

## What the game refuses to explain

**There is no lore debt.** The thing under the world does not mean anything. It
is not a fallen god, a sealed evil, or a lesson about hubris. It is *there*, it
is producing horrors, and the only frame the game offers is a man whose job it is
to get rid of it.

This is a scoping decision as much as a creative one: what gets authored is
**creatures and his reactions to them**, not documents, factions, or histories.
The content unit is a creature, not a chapter.

## The premise

Something beneath the earth — an infection, a cancer, a rot — is causing
everything above it to decay. It is making its way into the human world.

**Nobody has connected it.** The world is in a semi-normal state. There are
rumours; strange things have been happening. But there is no investigation, no
quarantine, no conspiracy — a town that simply does not know.

**The player is the first to uncover it**, and he uncovers it *by accident*. He
is not sent to investigate the house. He is sent to do his job at the house. The
game's central act is **inference**, not exploration: not "go find the anomaly"
but "go deal with the wasps," and then the floor is wrong.

Consequences of framing it this way, all of them good:

- The strange occurrences can be everywhere and unremarked. They are weather and
  bad luck and gossip until the player re-reads them. **The same authored town
  changes meaning because the player changed** — very cheap reactivity.
- Nobody can help and nobody is hiding anything. No cast of conspirators to write.
- "Why do you keep going down" answers itself: first trip it is a job; second
  trip it is *unfinished*, which is a professional problem, not a heroic one;
  then he is too far in. Nobody ever tells him to.

**There is no pre-existing descent. [SETTLED]** The dungeon is not a place that
was already there and is being revealed — **you make it as you go.** You find one
point of entry, you open it up, and it leads to the next. The map GROWS rather than
unfolds, and every floor exists because you opened the hole that leads to it.

That answers "how does a basement go sixteen levels down" by dissolving it: it
doesn't. Each POE is a way through to the next space, and the chain is the
descent.

## The protagonist

A normal guy working a blue-collar job — not a hero, a competent professional
who was sent. **A silent protagonist:** he never narrates and never explains. The
register comes from what he DOES and from the trade language around him, not from
a running commentary.

**The reference is MARIO, not a Metal Gear operative.** Mario is a plumber; a pipe
leads somewhere impossible; he goes down it. No exposition, no reluctance, no "what
*is* this place." The absurdity is load-bearing precisely because the game refuses
to treat it as absurd.

That distinction is the whole tone. A competent-and-AWARE protagonist (Snake) knows
he is in a strange situation and remarks on it — which makes the game *about* the
strangeness. A competent-and-INCURIOUS one just keeps working, and the horror goes
ambient: the player carries it alone, which is the gap the tone lives in.

(And the echo is not a coincidence worth ignoring: a plumber's trade is things
travelling through openings that connect places they should not. Mario's pipes are
points of entry.)

Why the trade earns its place:

- His job is *already* about things living in walls that should not be there. He
  does not need convincing that something is wrong — he needs convincing it is
  not rats. **His expertise is exactly what blinds him**, right up until it can't
  be explained.
- It gives a diegetic reason for the toolkit. He has no magic; he has
  **equipment**. Poison, traps, smoke, bait, sprayers. A projectile kit that is
  not guns, instantly readable, and it gets grotesque as it escalates — the same
  tools doing worse and worse things.
- "Extermination stats" are the character sheet. His competence IS his level, and
  it is funny and grim that the same numbers scale from roaches to whatever is at
  the bottom.

**What he wants: to be the best exterminator of all time. [SETTLED]** That is the
whole motivation, and it is sincere. He is not brave, not curious, and not
investigating — this is the biggest job anyone has ever been handed and he intends
to do it *well*. The apocalypse is a career opportunity.

It is a shonen protagonist's ambition ("I'll be the best") applied to pest control,
and it does real work:

- The descent needs no horror motivation. He goes down because the job is down
  there.
- **Levelling is characterisation, not just a system.** Extermination points are
  his skill, and getting better at exterminating is the literal thing he wants.
- It is why he goes back up: upgrades, supplies, selling loot — a professional
  restocking between jobs. Standard hub-world logic, and completely in character.

**He never cracks. [SETTLED]** That is the joke: the most stoic and determined
exterminator alive, who does not think twice about what he is exterminating. He is
the same man at the bottom as at the top. No breakdown, no moment of realisation,
no speech — because there is nothing to crack about. He got the job done. The final
image is a man who is now, verifiably, the best exterminator of all time.

## THE MECHANIC: seal the point of entry

**The point of entry is the spawner.** This is the game's title, its objective,
and its verb, and they are the same thing.

- A level's goal is not "kill everything" — it is **find and seal the holes they
  are coming in through.** There may be several per level.
- Sealing one stops that flow. The floor's pressure drops as you close them, so
  each level has a **self-balancing difficulty curve inside it**.
- It makes the swarm *pressure* rather than a checklist. You are doing a job while
  being attacked, which is exactly the fiction.
- **It is what an exterminator actually does.** Exclusion work is real: you do not
  kill your way out, you find how they are getting in and you close it.
- It escalates perfectly. Early: a crack in a foundation, a gap in a vent. Later:
  something that is not a hole in any normal sense. **The last one is the source,
  and the ending is the same verb as the tutorial.**
- **Sealing a POE opens the way onward.** They are not just spawners to shut off —
  each one is the passage to the next space. The chain of them IS the descent.

### The gadget — why the floors are different every time

A little robot you send into a space ahead of you. It clears the area and exposes
the nearest point of entry. **The floor is GENERATED when the gadget is used**, and
it is generated once: it does not re-roll every time you walk back through. The
space is unmapped until the robot maps it, and then it is a place.

This is the load-bearing trick of the whole production model:

- It makes procedural generation **diegetic**. The floors differ because nobody has
  ever been down there and the robot is what finds the way — not because the game
  is shuffling rooms.
- It makes generated space **persistent**, so a floor is somewhere you know rather
  than somewhere disposable. Solves the thing that makes roguelite floors feel
  weightless.
- It is a **pacing gate**. Sending the robot is a deliberate act, so the player
  chooses when to open the next space rather than stumbling into it.
- It is more equipment for a man who solves problems with equipment, and it can be
  upgraded like everything else he owns.

## The loop

```
into the house
  → send the gadget ahead; it clears a space and exposes a point of entry
     (the floor GENERATES here, once, and stays that way)
  → hunt and seal the POEs on this floor, under fire; the floor quiets
  → take what the killing pays, find loot
  → the sealed POE is the way onward
  → rest point: level up, or teleport back to the surface
  → town: shop, upgrade gear and materials, unlocks
  → deeper
```

**Structure is Diablo 1 / Souls, not roguelike.** Persistent character,
persistent progression, nothing resets between trips. The "-ish" is only the
*rhythm* of descend-extract-restock.

- **Rest points are bonfires** — level up, and teleport to the top of the house.
  Later, potentially, to other places in town.
- **What brings you back up is incentive, not inventory limits.** Unlocks, tools,
  upgrades and buyers exist only in town — standard hub-world pull, and exactly
  what a professional restocking between jobs would do. No bag-capacity nagging.
- **Two currencies, and they stay separate. [SETTLED]** They measure different
  things and are not interchangeable:
  - **Extermination points** (name TBD) — earned by killing, and they are his
    *skill*. This is the souls-equivalent: what you spend to level up.
  - **US dollars** — earned from loot, and they are his *material wealth*. This is
    what the hardware store takes.

### Death [SETTLED]

Deliberately not punishing. **You go back to the last rest point and drop a
fraction of the money you were carrying.** Experience and levels are never taken
away — what you learned, you keep; what you were carrying, you can lose. The
setback is a bad day at work, not a lost run.

## Combat

- **Mouse-aimed, click to fire.** Decoupled from facing: the sprite flips left or
  right; the aim is wherever the cursor is. Twin-stick convention, instantly
  readable.
- **A tool hits an AREA, not a target.** [SETTLED] This is the load-bearing
  decision, and it is what makes swarms playable: one input clears everything
  inside a shape, rather than one input killing one thing. A game of large
  crowds where each shot kills a single enemy cannot keep up with its own
  swarm — the player drowns, and the fantasy of clearing a room dies with it.
  Area size is one of the things upgrades change.
- **Melee and ranged are the same primitive.** The area is identical; only where
  it appears differs. A close-range tool puts its area beside the player in the
  aim direction; a ranged tool sends one out along it. There is no separate
  melee system, and there should never be one.
- **A few upgradable tools, fired manually.** Build depth in the Vampire
  Survivors sense, but nothing fires on a timer. Because every shot is chosen,
  a tool can be slower and heavier than an auto-firing one — the fogger that
  clears a room is fair when you picked the moment.
- **Bi-directional sprites only.** Half the art of a 4-way game, no facing state
  machine.
- **Known risk of this control scheme:** decoupled aim makes backing-away-while-
  shooting optimal. Enemy design must answer it — things that close fast, flank,
  or punish standing still.

## The town [SETTLED in shape]

A **Mother 1 / Earthbound town** — slice-of-life, realistic, unremarkable. A bar,
a hardware store (where he gets supplies), his own house, and the other things a
town has.

**Why he cannot leave:** the anomalies have got enough attention that the town is
**quarantined**. Police and military block the roads. He is not staying out of
duty or curiosity — he is stuck here, and the job is in here with him.

That does a lot of work cheaply: it bounds the map without a fence of invisible
walls, it explains why nobody outside is helping, and it means the authorities
know *something* is wrong without knowing what — they have cordoned the symptom
and missed the cause entirely.

## The bestiary IS the escalation

Ants → mice → worse → demons. This single ladder is simultaneously:

- the difficulty curve
- the art progression
- the tonal drift from comedy to horror
- the content-scaling model (a new creature is a week's work and buys real play)
- the natural end of entry one — **whatever tier this game stops at is where the
  sequel starts**

A boss every few levels. The final boss is the thing that has been corrupting the
world.

## Aesthetic

**GBC / Link to the Past / Mother 1 — as a visual language, not a hardware
constraint.** Modern resolution, modern field of view. Limited palettes, chunky
readable shapes, thick outlines, minimal shading, low frame counts.

- **Minimal but effective.** A creature is a silhouette plus two colours. Mother
  1's enemies are memorable because they are simple and strange, not detailed.
- **The "amateurish" quality is deliberate and hard to fake.** Mother 1 looks
  naive because Itoi was not a game artist. Bold simple shapes, slightly-off
  proportions, and *not polishing*. The instinct to add detail would kill it.
- Set in the US, which is what the LttP/Crystal palette is doing.

## Why it can ship in a year

**The engine already carries the hard part** (`engines/engine/`): rendering,
animation, audio, camera, collision, flow-field pathfinding, steering, tilemap +
renderer, sprites, fonts, UI, physics. Prison-escape's own `main.cpp` is 207
lines, which is the proof.

**Prison-escape is a REFERENCE, not a parent.** It already solved — for a
descending, wave-based, proc-genned action game — spawning, aggro/chase,
projectiles, rest spots, ladders, leveling, weapon XP, equipment, crafting,
inventory, pickups, save/load. Those problems are solved once and can be solved
again quickly, but they get **ported deliberately, one at a time, because the new
game needed them**. Forking wholesale would inherit ~1000 files of prison-specific
content to then delete, and its combat (melee-hitbox-first) and proc-gen (80×60
wave arenas) are not what this game wants anyway.

**And the art style was chosen partly because it is cheap.** Flat, minimal,
two-frame, two-direction. That is what makes a solo year plausible.

## It is a pilot

Intended as a showcase — the first of a potential series. Which means:

- **The exterminator and his job are the franchise**, not this house or this
  secret. Entry one should finish *this job* and leave the work continuing. Do
  not spend the whole premise.
- **Invest in what transfers**: the combat feel, the shop, the escalation gag, his
  voice. Not in this specific secret.
- The pest-control-versus-hell hook is the brand.

## Reference points

- **Tone:** Control (bureaucracy as horror delivery), Severance, Roadside Picnic,
  Papers Please
- **Register + art:** **Lisa: The Painful** (the closest single reference), OFF
  (a protagonist performing a task with total conviction in an unexplained
  world), Fear & Hunger, Hylics, Mother 1
- **Structure:** Diablo 1 (small hub, deep hole, cheap content), Noita (systems
  generate the content), Dungeons of Dredmor
- **Film:** Annihilation — biology doing blasphemous things, no explanation, no
  villain

## Open questions

1. **How much of a floor can be authored** within the generator — how many room
   templates a tier needs before it stops feeling repetitive, and whether
   set-piece rooms (a boss arena, the thing you remember) are hand-placed among
   the generated ones.
2. **What the first job looks like.** Not "what scares him into returning" — the
   POE chain answers that, because the job is simply not finished. But the first
   twenty minutes still has to teach the loop and land the tone, and what he is
   originally called out for (wasps? rats? a smell?) sets the whole opening.
3. **What the extermination-points currency is called.** "Extermination points" is
   a placeholder. It wants to be trade-flavoured and slightly wrong.
4. **What the gadget is called, and how far it upgrades.** It is load-bearing
   enough to deserve a good name.
5. **How the swarm reads at a modern FOV** with mouse aim — specifically whether
   backing-away-while-shooting needs an enemy-design answer early (see Combat).
6. **What the town's anomalies actually are** — the unremarked strange things the
   player walks past before he can read them.
